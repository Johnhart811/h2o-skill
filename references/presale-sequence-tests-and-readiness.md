# Presale Sequence, Certainty Levels, Tests & Readiness

Read this for Stages I, J, and (when diagnosing underperforming content)
part of Stage M of the main workflow.

---

## Stage J — Buyer Journey & Certainty

### Buyer Journey Stages
**Unaware → Problem Aware → Solution Aware → Provider Aware → Offer Aware →
Decision Ready**

Identify the audience's actual current stage and the *next* belief or piece
of information required to move them forward one stage — not five. Don't
force Decision-Ready content (feature/price comparisons) onto a
Problem-Unaware audience, and don't re-explain the category to a
Decision-Ready one; the first falls flat, the second reads as condescending.

### The Five Certainties
The prospect needs to progressively reach all five before buying feels
obvious. Identify which one(s) a specific piece of content is responsible
for — most single assets target one or two, not all five:

1. **Problem** — "I understand what's actually wrong."
2. **Importance** — "I understand why it matters."
3. **Solution** — "I understand what needs to be done."
4. **Provider** — "I believe they can do it."
5. **Action** — "I know what I should do next."

### The 11-Phase Presale Sequence
Use this to plan a full campaign, not force every single asset through all
eleven phases — each asset should have one clear job:

1. Attention — the prospect recognizes themselves.
2. Problem recognition — sometimes revealing the real problem isn't the one
   they thought.
3. Consequence — why continuing matters (grounded in the Do-Nothing test
   below, not manufactured fear).
4. Reframe — a better way of thinking about the problem.
5. Possibility — what becomes possible once solved correctly.
6. Mechanism — how the solution actually works.
7. Differentiation — criteria-based, never disparaging.
8. Proof — credible, real evidence.
9. Objection removal — the objections most relevant to this audience,
   pre-answered.
10. Decision — help them determine if it's actually right for them
    (including honestly saying who it's not for).
11. Action — a simple, obvious, low-pressure next step.

**Don't sell too early** — open in the customer's world (phases 1-3) so they
think "that's me" before "that's their product." **Not every piece needs a
hard CTA** — content that only advances one phase or one certainty level
still earns its place.

## Stage I — The Nine Tests

Run these before finalizing positioning for a new offer, or when something
already live isn't converting. They exist to catch a weak *offer* or a
genuine gap before it gets disguised with better copy — copy cannot fix an
offer problem (see Offer Diagnosis in
`references/offer-and-decision-criteria.md`).

1. **Why Not** — "What are the strongest reasons a rational customer would
   choose not to buy?" Generate at least 10. For each, classify: marketing
   problem, offer problem, trust problem, price/value problem, timing
   problem, fit problem, or proof problem. Address what's solvable through
   content; state plainly what isn't.
2. **Why You** — "If they want the outcome, why should they choose this
   provider?" If the honest answer is vague, don't reach for stronger
   adjectives — go back and find (or help the operator articulate) a genuine
   differentiator.
3. **Why Now** — "Why act now rather than later?" If there's no legitimate
   answer, don't manufacture urgency — lean on a different phase of the
   sequence instead (certainty, proof, fit).
4. **Do Nothing** — "What happens if they do nothing?" Show realistic
   consequences without exaggeration. Also identify situations where doing
   nothing is genuinely reasonable for some prospects — the goal is decision
   clarity, not fear.
5. **Better Alternative** — "What would a rational customer choose instead?"
   Don't assume this offer wins by default; find out honestly. If an
   alternative is genuinely better for some customers, say so — it improves
   credibility and naturally qualifies the audience.
6. **Customer Fit** — "Who should NOT buy this?" Define poor-fit customers
   where appropriate. This keeps the marketing from trying to persuade
   everybody, which is itself a trust signal (see Trust Architecture).
7. **Promise Integrity** — re-run the Promise → Mechanism → Delivery →
   Evidence → Outcome chain from the Offer Diagnosis; flag any weak link.
8. **Message Saturation** — "Would this message stand out beside 20
   competitors saying similar things?" If not, diagnose why: generic
   message, weak positioning, market-owned language, missing customer
   insight, or an unclear mechanism (see Message Saturation Analysis).
9. **Selling Remaining** — "After reading this, what would I still need to
   explain in a sales call?" Every answer is a candidate for more content:
   an FAQ, a proof point, a landing-page section, a comparison, a
   demonstration, or explicit objection handling. The aim is to keep moving
   explanations upstream, permanently.

## Presale Readiness Score

When enough information exists, score the system 0–100 across: problem
clarity, desire clarity, priority, solution understanding, mechanism
clarity, differentiation, proof, trust, risk reduction, objection coverage,
alternative comparison, delivery clarity, decision clarity, CTA clarity.

| Range | Meaning |
|---|---|
| 0–30 | Cold / poorly prepared |
| 31–50 | Problem aware |
| 51–70 | Solution aware |
| 71–85 | Provider / offer aware |
| 86–100 | Strong buying readiness |

This score is diagnostic, not a prediction of actual conversion — use it to
show the operator where the system is weakest, not as a guarantee.

## Pre-Sale Failure Analysis

Use this when something is already live and underperforming, to localize
where the system broke rather than guessing at a rewrite. Work through each
mode and mark which applies:

- **Attention failure** — they never noticed it.
- **Relevance failure** — they didn't recognize themselves in it.
- **Problem failure** — they didn't believe the problem mattered.
- **Desire failure** — the outcome wasn't compelling enough.
- **Priority failure** — something else mattered more right now.
- **Belief failure** — they didn't believe the solution could work.
- **Mechanism failure** — they didn't understand *why* it should work.
- **Trust failure** — they didn't trust the provider.
- **Proof failure** — not enough evidence existed.
- **Differentiation failure** — they couldn't see why this was different.
- **Risk failure** — perceived risk was too high.
- **Comparison failure** — they couldn't tell which option was better for
  them.
- **Offer failure** — the offer itself wasn't compelling enough (traces back
  to Stage A).
- **Delivery failure** — they didn't understand what happens after purchase.
- **Decision failure** — too many options or too much uncertainty.
- **Action failure** — they didn't know what to do next.

This analysis must produce a specific recommendation tied to the failure
mode(s) found — not a generic "make it more compelling."
