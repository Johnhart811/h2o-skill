# Offer Diagnosis & Decision Criteria Engineering

Read this for Stage A (Offer Diagnosis) and Stage G's criteria half of the
main workflow. Run Stage A first, always — diagnosing the customer before
confirming the offer itself holds together is how a weak offer gets
disguised as a copy problem.

---

## Offer Diagnosis

Understand the offer before marketing it. Establish, labeling each per the
Evidence Labeling rule in SKILL.md:

- What is actually being sold?
- Who is it genuinely for?
- **Who is it NOT suitable for?** (Answering this honestly is itself a trust
  signal later, and it prevents the marketing from trying to persuade
  everyone.)
- What problem does it solve, and what outcome does it create?
- How does it actually work, and how is it delivered?
- What genuinely makes it different?
- What proof currently exists for its claims?
- What does it cost, and what are the real constraints (time, effort,
  prerequisites)?
- What does the customer need to do, and what happens after purchase?

### The Promise Integrity Chain

Test whether these five actually line up:

**Promise → Mechanism → Delivery → Evidence → Outcome**

- **Promise** — what's being claimed
- **Mechanism** — why the claim should be true (the actual how)
- **Delivery** — whether the business can actually execute the mechanism
  consistently
- **Evidence** — whether proof exists that it has worked
- **Outcome** — whether the claimed result is what customers actually get

If any link is weak or missing, don't paper over it in the copy. Classify
what's actually broken:

| Classification | What it means |
|---|---|
| Marketing problem | The offer is fine, it just isn't being communicated well |
| Positioning problem | The offer is being compared on the wrong criteria or to the wrong alternative |
| Proof problem | The mechanism and delivery are real, but evidence is missing or weak |
| Offer problem | The thing being sold doesn't actually solve the problem well enough |
| Delivery problem | The mechanism is sound in theory but execution is inconsistent |
| Trust problem | Everything checks out, but the prospect has no reason yet to believe it |
| Pricing/value problem | The price and the delivered value aren't aligned for this audience |

**Do not attempt to solve an offer, delivery, proof, or pricing problem with
better copy.** If Stage A surfaces one of these, say so to the operator
plainly before proceeding to content — this is more useful to them than a
polished piece of marketing built on a shaky foundation, because that
content will convert once and then generate complaints, refunds, or churn.

## Decision Criteria Engineering

This is where the "why should someone choose this" question gets answered
honestly rather than defensively.

**Don't start with:** "Why are we better?"
**Start with:** "What should a smart customer actually evaluate when
choosing a solution to this problem?"

1. List the criteria that genuinely matter for this category — pulled from
   what real customers care about, not from what makes the offer look best.
   Typical candidates: outcome, reliability, implementation ease, speed,
   support, expertise, simplicity, total cost (not just sticker price), risk,
   convenience, durability, measurable performance. Not all apply to every
   offer — pick the ones that are actually relevant here.
2. **Do not invent a criterion whose only purpose is making this offer win.**
   If a criterion doesn't hold up as something a smart, skeptical customer
   would actually care about, drop it.
3. Evaluate the offer honestly against each real criterion — including where
   it's genuinely average or weaker than an alternative. That honesty is
   what makes the strong criteria credible.

This reframes the decision from *"which provider should I pick?"* (a
comparison the prospect has to do the work to believe) to *"what actually
matters when choosing the right solution — and does this option hold up
against that?"* (a frame the prospect can verify for themselves, which is
why it's more durable than a direct comparative claim).

Once criteria are set, this feeds directly into the Unique Mechanism and
Alternative Superiority sections in
`references/objection-competitive-trust-risk.md` — the criteria are the
yardstick; the mechanism and alternative comparison are what gets measured
against it.
