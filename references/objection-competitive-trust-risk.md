# Objection Mapping, Competitive Intelligence, Trust & Risk Architecture

Read this for Stages F, G, and H of the main workflow.

---

## Stage F — Objection Architecture

Build a complete objection map. For each objection relevant to this
audience, work the full chain rather than a one-line rebuttal:

**Objection → Root cause → Evidence needed → Message → Proof → Next belief**

| Objection type | Typical form |
|---|---|
| Price | "Too expensive." |
| Trust | "Can I trust you?" |
| Risk | "What if it doesn't work?" |
| Competence | "Can you actually do this?" |
| Timing | "Maybe later." |
| Effort | "This sounds like too much work." |
| Complexity | "I don't understand how this works." |
| Comparison | "Why shouldn't I choose someone else?" |
| Status quo | "Maybe what I'm doing now is good enough." |
| Self-doubt | "Maybe I can't make this work for me." |
| Decision paralysis | "I need to think about it." |
| Implementation | "How do I actually get this running?" |
| Commitment | "What if I'm not ready to commit to this?" |
| Switching costs | "What do I lose by moving away from what I have?" |
| Previous bad experience | "I got burned by something like this before." |

Don't just answer a price objection with "it's not too expensive." Establish
what the prospect should actually be weighing the cost against: value,
outcome, total cost (not sticker price), cost of delay, cost of failure, and
the realistic alternatives — see Alternative Superiority below.

### Presell the Delivery Experience
Objections about effort and risk often dissolve when the *process* is made
concrete rather than the outcome alone. Answer, wherever relevant: what
happens right after they say yes, what they need to provide and how much
time it takes, realistic duration, what communication to expect and how
often, what happens if something changes mid-way, what support exists, and
what "done" actually looks like. This reduces resistance before purchase
more effectively than reassurance language does, because it's concrete.

## Stage G — Competitive & Market Intelligence

Research the real competitive environment when current information is
available; label anything that can't be verified as UNKNOWN or NEEDS
RESEARCH rather than guessing.

### What to map
- **Direct competitors** — substantially similar solutions.
- **Indirect competitors** — different solution types addressing the same
  problem.
- **DIY** — the customer doing it themselves.
- **Cheaper alternatives** — lower-cost attempts at the same outcome.
- **Familiar alternatives** — existing solutions the customer already
  trusts.
- **Temporary fixes / workarounds.**
- **Delay / doing nothing** — inertia is often the real competitor.

For named competitors, investigate where accessible: offers, positioning,
headlines, hooks, claims, proof, pricing, guarantees, landing pages, social
content, ad presence, visible campaign themes, review patterns (complaints
and praise), posting frequency and timing.

**Keep three things separate at all times:**
- **Observed fact** — what's directly visible.
- **Inference** — a reasonable read of what's visible.
- **Strategic interpretation** — what it means for this offer's positioning.

Never present inference or interpretation as observed fact. Never name
competitors directly in content, and never make an unverifiable comparative
claim.

### Message Saturation Analysis
Identify what the market repeatedly says — "professional," "affordable,"
"quality," "trusted," "best service," "customer focused," "fast," "reliable."
A message can be entirely true and still be strategically useless if
everyone in the category says it; the audience has stopped hearing it.
Determine:

- **Market-owned language** — messages everybody uses (avoid leading with
  these; they read as invisible).
- **Market gaps** — what customers need to hear that competitors aren't
  addressing.
- **Unanswered questions** — the ones competitors avoid.
- **Under-served frustrations** — problems customers repeatedly mention that
  nobody's directly addressing.
- **Unclaimed positioning** — a meaningful space this operator can credibly
  occupy.

The goal isn't difference for its own sake — it's being **meaningfully
different where the customer actually cares**, which is why this stage feeds
directly from the real criteria established in
`references/offer-and-decision-criteria.md`.

### Unique Mechanism
Avoid empty differentiation ("we care more," "we're passionate," "we're the
best," "exceptional quality") — invisible to an audience that's heard every
competitor say the same thing. Use the structure: **"Most common approaches
do X. We do Y, because Z."** The mechanism must be real — grounded in
process, method, technology, expertise, implementation, service model,
product design, delivery, support, or risk management — something a typical
alternative would have to structurally change their business to copy.

### Alternative Superiority (Honest Comparison)
Never blindly attack alternatives. Answer instead: **"For this customer, in
this situation, what makes this solution a stronger fit than the realistic
alternatives?"** — measured against the real criteria from Stage A/G, not
invented ones. Include, honestly:

- Where this offer genuinely wins.
- Where a competitor genuinely wins.
- Where DIY genuinely wins.
- Where a cheaper option genuinely wins.
- Where doing nothing may actually be the rational choice for some people.
- Where this offer is simply not the right fit.

This honesty is what makes the comparison credible instead of promotional —
and it directly supports the Customer Fit test in
`references/presale-sequence-tests-and-readiness.md`.

## Stage H — Trust & Risk Architecture

### Trust Architecture
Trust isn't one thing — build it through multiple concrete levers:
competence, honesty, transparency, consistency, proof, specificity, process
visibility, expectation-setting, appropriate vulnerability, demonstrated
understanding of the problem, clearly stated limitations, and clear fit
criteria (who this is and isn't for). A genuinely powerful trust signal,
when it's true, is simply: **"This isn't for everyone."** Saying it costs
nothing when honest and buys real credibility.

### Proof Architecture
Map every major claim to real evidence: case studies, results, before/after
examples, testimonials, reviews, data, credentials, process transparency,
genuine guarantees, demonstrations of expertise. **Never invent proof.** If a
major claim has no evidence behind it, flag it explicitly as a **PROOF GAP**
and note what evidence should be collected before the claim is made more
prominently — don't quietly drop the flag once it's raised.

### Risk Architecture
Identify the specific perceived risks for this offer and audience:
financial, performance, time, reputational, implementation/complexity,
switching, disappointment, general uncertainty. Reduce them through proof,
demonstrations, a clear process, genuine guarantees, transparent
expectations, clear boundaries, examples, and phased implementation where
relevant. **Never promise elimination of risk that still exists** — reduce
it honestly, don't claim it away.
