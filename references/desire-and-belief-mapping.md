# Desire & Belief Mapping

Read this for Stages B, C, D, and E of the main workflow. Don't skip layers
— the surface desire is rarely what actually moves someone to act, and the
identity/hidden layers usually carry the real pull.

---

## Stage B — Customer & Environment Diagnosis

Build the customer from evidence wherever possible — don't invent a
fictional persona just because the format expects one. Label everything per
the Evidence Labeling rule (KNOWN / ASSUMED / UNKNOWN / NEEDS RESEARCH /
NEEDS OPERATOR INPUT). Establish: current situation, desired situation,
immediate problem, deeper problem underneath it, fears and anxieties,
decision criteria they'd naturally use, buying triggers, reasons for delay,
and both emotional and practical consequences of the problem persisting.

Then apply the Decision Environment framing from SKILL.md concretely to this
customer: what else is competing for their attention, budget, and priority
right now? What other advice or messages are they currently receiving about
this same problem?

---

## Stage C — The Six Layers of Desire

Work through all six for the offer and audience in front of you. Don't stop
at the first one that sounds plausible.

1. **Surface desire** — what they'd say out loud if asked. ("I want a new
   website.")
2. **Functional desire** — what they actually want the product/service to
   *do*. ("I want the site to generate leads without me manually replying to
   every enquiry.")
3. **Emotional desire** — how they want to *feel*. ("I want to stop feeling
   behind everyone else in my industry.")
4. **Identity desire** — who they want to become or how they want to be
   seen. ("I want to be the contractor people describe as 'the professional
   one'.")
5. **Outcome desire** — what actually changes in their life/business once
   the problem is solved. ("I want to stop personally chasing every lead so
   I can spend evenings with my family.")
6. **Hidden desire** — what they want but rarely say publicly, sometimes not
   even to themselves. ("I want to prove to people who doubted me that this
   works.") Handle with care — surface it in tone and implication, not by
   stating it bluntly back at the reader, which reads as presumptuous.

### Desire Hierarchy
- **Primary desire** — the one most likely to actually drive the decision.
  Pick one; don't mix desires within a single piece of content. A message
  aimed at three desires reads as a pitch; aimed at one, it reads as
  certainty.
- **Supporting desires** — reinforce the primary one; can appear as
  secondary proof, never as a competing reason-to-buy.
- **Conflicting desires** — pull the prospect in another direction (below).

## Conflicting Desires

Name the specific pair relevant to this audience — don't gloss over the
tension:

- "I want more customers" vs. "I don't want to spend more."
- "I want professional help" vs. "I should do it myself."
- "I want this solved quickly" vs. "I don't want to rush the decision."
- "I want quality" vs. "I want the cheapest option."
- "I want change" vs. "I don't want the disruption change requires."
- "I want certainty" vs. "I don't want to spend time researching."

Never simply tell the prospect which desire they should have — that reads
as pressure. Resolve the conflict by supplying **context, criteria, evidence,
reframing, prioritisation, or risk reduction** so the prospect can see the
trade-off clearly and resolve it themselves. Determine which specific
conflict is actually preventing movement for this audience before writing —
addressing the wrong one wastes the content.

## Scattered Focus → Decision Concentration

Identify everything currently competing for this prospect's attention and
decision: other problems competing for focus, other purchases competing for
budget, other solutions they're weighing, advice they're receiving,
competitor messages already in front of them, and what's causing outright
decision fatigue.

The content's job is **decision concentration** — moving the prospect from
many competing possibilities toward one clearly understood problem, one
clearly understood opportunity, and clear criteria for evaluating a
solution. This is not the same as narrowing their options for them by
force; it's giving them enough clarity that narrowing happens naturally.

## Stage D — Frustration & Failed-Attempt Analysis

Go deeper than a generic pain-point list. For every significant prior
attempt this audience has likely made, work through:

1. What did they try?
2. Why did they try it?
3. What happened?
4. Why did it fail or disappoint — the actual mechanism, not just "it didn't
   work"?
5. What did they conclude from the failure?
6. What belief did that failure create? (Commonly: "nothing works," "I've
   tried this before," "everyone promises the same thing," "it's probably a
   waste of money," "I'll just keep doing what I'm doing.")
7. How is the proposed solution structurally different — not a better
   version of the same approach, but a different mechanism entirely?

Address the **mechanism of the previous failure** directly in content, not
just a promise of better results — that's what makes the reader feel
understood rather than pitched to, and it's what separates "trying again"
from "trying something fundamentally different" in their mind.

Also identify daily-level frustrations connected to the problem: what wastes
their time specifically, what costs them money in a way they can name, what
embarrasses them, what uncertainty they've simply learned to tolerate.
Connect the solution to removal of the *specific* frustration named, not a
generic adjacent benefit.

## Stage E — Belief Map

### Limiting Beliefs Inventory
Identify which of these (or an audience-specific equivalent) is doing the
most damage for this offer and audience — pick one to target per piece, not
all of them at once:

"I can't afford this." · "People like me don't need this." · "I can do it
myself." · "It's probably too complicated." · "I've already tried something
similar." · "Nothing has worked before." · "It's probably too good to be
true." · "I need to think about it." · "I need to compare more options." ·
"I'll do it later." · "I don't have enough information." · "I don't know
who to trust."

For the chosen belief: state it plainly (without attacking the reader for
holding it), then replace it with a corrected belief backed by a reason,
mechanism, or specific example — arguing against a belief head-on triggers
defensiveness; changing the context around it is what actually moves it.

### The Belief Ladder
Before someone buys, they climb through these beliefs, roughly in order.
Identify which rungs a specific piece of content is responsible for moving —
most single assets move 1–3 rungs, not all fourteen:

1. My problem is real.
2. My current approach is insufficient.
3. The problem matters / continuing has a real cost.
4. A better approach exists.
5. That better approach makes sense.
6. This *type* of solution (the vehicle) can actually work.
7. This particular mechanism makes sense.
8. This provider understands the problem.
9. This provider can execute the solution.
10. The risk is acceptable.
11. The opportunity is worth acting on.
12. The offer fits my specific situation.
13. Waiting is less attractive than moving forward.
14. The next step is clear.

Map a campaign's content pieces against these rungs so nothing gets
re-argued unnecessarily (multiple pieces stuck re-proving rung 1) and
nothing gets skipped (jumping to rung 13 with an audience still stuck on
rung 3).
